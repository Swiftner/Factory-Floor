# Jobs To Be Done

Load when customer motivation is unclear — why they buy, why they don't, why they
left, or what to build next.

You know Christensen, Moesta, and Ulwick. This file is how Factory Floor holds them.

JTBD sits **underneath** every other pillar. You can't find the constraint without
knowing what job the customer hired you for.

---

## The mindset

- **Nobody buys a product. They hire it to make progress**, and they fire something
  else to do it. What they fire tells you the real competitive set — usually a
  spreadsheet or a manual process, not the startups the founder lists.
- **Non-consumption is the biggest competitor.** Most potential customers have
  quietly accepted the problem is unsolvable.
- **Jobs are more emotional than functional.** The functional job is table stakes;
  the emotional and social dimensions decide the deal and carry the premium.
  Founders stop at functional — push past it every time.
- **A better feature is rarely the highest-leverage move.** Push and Pull must
  exceed Anxiety and Habit. Reducing anxiety (a guarantee, a testimonial from
  someone like them, a free pilot) or reducing habit (showing the hidden cost of
  today, making migration effortless) is usually cheaper and works faster. **More
  features can increase anxiety.**
- **Interviews are about situations, never features or hypotheticals.** "Did you"
  beats "would you," always.
- **Get the struggling moment or you got nothing.** Not general dissatisfaction — a
  concrete event with a date. It is both the origin of every purchase and the exact
  words your marketing needs.

**Every struggling moment is a Category Entry Point.** Mapping struggling moments
and mapping CEPs are the same exercise on the same list → `pillar-sharp.md`.

## Switch interviews

**The mechanism:** reconstruct the buying timeline — first thought, passive
looking, the trigger event, active looking, the narrowing event, deciding,
first use, satisfaction. What people *think* they want is worthless; what *caused*
them to act is everything.

**Three layers of language, and only the third is useful:**

- Under 10 min — **pablum**: "It was fine." "We needed something better."
- ~20 min — **fantasy/nightmare**: emotional but imprecise. "Those meetings were SO long!"
- 30-60 min — **causal**: "That meeting ran 30 minutes late, I missed my kid's
  game, and I thought: I can't keep doing this."

**An interview that stays in the pablum layer produced nothing.** Don't let the
founder count it.

**Questions by force:**

- **Push** — "What was happening that made you start looking?" / "What was
  frustrating about how you handled this before?" / "Did you try something else first?"
- **Pull** — "What did you hope it would do for you?" / "What made you think this
  was the right fit?" / "When you say 'simple,' what makes it feel that way?"
- **Anxiety** — "What worried you about switching?" / "Was there a point you almost
  didn't go through with it?" / "What could go wrong?"
- **Habit** — "What made it hard to leave what you were doing?" / "What works about
  your current setup?" / "What do you miss about the old way?"

**Never ask "why" repeatedly** — it triggers rationalization. Use: "Tell me more
about that." "Give me an example." "Help me understand." "What happened next?"

**Ask what's hard, not what's good.** People expound on "no" and give pablum on
"yes." "What's good?" returns "it just needs to be easy."

**Sample:** 8-12 interviews yield reliable patterns; past 12 adds noise. Interview
people who switched or churned within the **last 90 days** — memory degrades fast.
Stop when new interviews echo old ones. Debrief for roughly as long as you interviewed.

**In B2B:** interview at least three roles from the buying center (budget owner,
end user, evaluator, gatekeeper). Anxiety is amplified by career risk and security
review; timelines run 6-18 months from first thought to purchase.

**Wasted interviews:** asking about features instead of situations · hypotheticals
instead of behavior · skipping emotional context · interviewing buyers but not
users · treating it as a survey instead of following the emotional energy ·
interviewing people who bought too long ago.

## Deciding what to build

Map the job end to end, independent of any solution — the map is the same whether
the customer uses you, a competitor, or nothing. **The steps where existing
solutions fail are the opportunity.** Build where you create unique value;
integrate where something already serves the job well.

Capture desired outcomes in the customer's terms, always in the direction of
*less*: less time, less effort, less risk, less likelihood of the bad thing.

Score each on importance and current satisfaction. **High importance + low
satisfaction = under-served = where to build.** High satisfaction on a
low-importance outcome means the category is over-serving — that's a
strip-features-and-lower-price opening, not a gap.

**Without survey data** (most startups), use a qualitative proxy after 8-12
interviews. Stack-rank by: how strong is the push? how many people surfaced it
independently? are they building workarounds? does it pass the not-not test?

## From JTBD to positioning

Positioning starts with the one question only JTBD answers: **what were they using
before they hired you?** Ask churned or switched customers: *"If we disappeared,
what would you do?"*

In order: competitive alternatives (what they actually used) → your unique
attributes → the value those deliver, with proof → who cares most about that value
→ the category frame that makes the value obvious.

**Messaging order:** lead with the struggling moment, not the feature. Show the
outcome in their language. Address the anxiety directly. Then prove it.

**Use their words, untranslated.** Founders reflexively convert customer language
into category jargon and lose everything that made it land.

## Demand generation vs. demand capture

Two different populations. Confusing them is a top go-to-market error.

**Capture** — they've had the struggling moment and are searching now. SEO on
problem terms, review sites, comparison pages, case studies, fast trials. Physical
availability work.

**Generation** — they'll have the moment later, or have had it without escalating
to a search. Content at CEPs, thought leadership, community, talks, partnerships,
distinctive assets. Mental availability work.

**The trap:** almost every startup does only capture. It's measurable, converts
fast, and feels like real marketing — but the pool of active searchers is tiny
compared to the market. **Capture-only caps growth at search volume.**

| Situation | Priority |
|---|---|
| Product/market fit, flat growth | **Generation** — the searcher pool is tapped. Expand it. |
| Strong awareness, low conversion | **Capture** — they know you and can't find, try, or buy. |
| Pre product/market fit | Neither. Validate the job. |
| Scaling | Both — roughly 60-70% generation, 30-40% capture, more as you move upmarket. |

Interviews feed both, but from different phases: the early timeline (first thought,
the trigger) gives you generation material; the later timeline (active looking)
gives you capture material. **Most teams mine only the late phases and wonder why
their content generates no new pipeline.**
