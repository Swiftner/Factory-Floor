# Playbooks — the mechanical procedures

Everything here is **PROCEDURE mode**: checkable, transmissible, and the place a
struggling founder gains most. Run them exactly. Don't soften, don't offer
alternatives, don't ask whether they'd like to.

Name the protocol out loud when you start one. Founders learn the names by hearing
them used.

---

## Funnel break scan

**"Walk me through your last 10 signups / demos / deals. Where did each one end up?"**

Refuse the summary. Listen for where they go vague — that's the break.

| Can't say… | Break at |
|---|---|
| How people find us, how many arrive | Acquisition |
| How many actually try it | Activation |
| How many pay | Revenue |
| How many are still here at 60 days | Retention |
| Why people left, what they went to | Fit |

They will jump to a diagnosis before finishing. Hold: *"Before we get there — walk
me through those 10."*

---

## Napkin test

Five minutes, on paper, in front of them.

1. Three-year revenue goal — the honest minimum to be worth doing.
2. Annual revenue per customer. Price × frequency.
3. Required customers = goal ÷ revenue per customer.
4. Realistically reachable market. Not TAM — people with this job, in segments they
   can actually reach, at that price.

**Required > reachable → the model is dead.** Say it plainly. The fix is never
"market harder": change the price, the segment, or the model, then re-run the math
in front of them.

Re-run annually at scale. Math that worked at $500K ARR often fails at $5M — market
saturating at the price point, CAC rising faster than LTV, best segment fully
penetrated. Then the fix is upmarket, product expansion into adjacent jobs (most
capital-efficient), or a different pricing model.

---

## Five tests — before any code

1. **Not-not.** "Is it not okay for your customer to NOT have this?" A shrug means
   nice-to-have. Nice-to-haves die on contact with a budget.
2. **Job.** "Name the job in the customer's exact words." Paraphrase = hasn't listened.
3. **Lean Canvas**, in risk order, starting from **existing alternatives** — what
   they use today — never from the idea. Riskiest assumption is almost always "does
   anyone have this badly enough to pay?"
4. **Napkin** — above.
5. **Mafia Offer** — below.

---

## Mafia Offer

> "We help [segment] achieve [outcome] within [timeframe], unlike [alternative]
> which [limitation]. [Price]. If we don't deliver, [risk reversal]."

Must clear **3× better** than the existing alternative on the dimension they care
about most. Not 10% — obviously, dramatically better. Less won't beat switching costs.

Test with a real ask: a deposit, a pre-order, an LOI. Not "would you buy this?" —
**"here's the invoice."**

**Desirability → Feasibility → Viability.** The MVP delivers a validated offer. The
offer comes first.

## Switch interviews

Reconstruct the timeline: first thought → passive looking → trigger event → active
looking → narrowing event → decision → first use.

**Three layers, and only the third is worth anything.** Under 10 min you get pablum
("it was fine"). Around 20, emotion without precision. At 30–60 min, causal detail:
*"that meeting ran 30 minutes late, I missed my kid's game, and I thought — I can't
keep doing this."* An interview that stayed in the pablum layer produced nothing;
don't let them count it.

**By force:**
- **Push** — "What was happening that made you start looking?" / "Did you try
  something else first?"
- **Pull** — "What did you hope it would do?" / "When you say 'simple,' what makes
  it feel that way?"
- **Anxiety** — "What worried you about switching?" / "Was there a point you almost
  didn't?"
- **Habit** — "What made it hard to leave the old way?" / "What do you miss?"

**Never ask "why" repeatedly** — it triggers rationalization. Use "tell me more,"
"give me an example," "what happened next."

**Ask what's hard, not what's good.** People expound on "no" and give pablum on "yes."

**8–12 interviews.** Past 12 adds noise. Only people who switched or churned in the
last 90 days. In B2B, at least three roles from the buying center.

**The 5-minute canvas**, after *every* conversation:

```
JOB / TRIGGER / OLD WAY
PUSH / PULL / ANXIETY / HABIT
OUTCOME (their words) / EMOTIONAL / SOCIAL
```

8–12 canvases and the patterns are real. If they contradict the founder's
assumptions, the assumptions change.

## CEP mapping

A Category Entry Point is the situation that makes someone start looking. Struggling
moments and CEPs are the same list.

**1 — List them (30 min).** From your canvases, from observation, from what
competitors' pages describe. Aim for **8–15**. Fewer is too narrow; more is
sub-variations.

Format is a situation in the buyer's language, never a topic or a job title. Not
"pipeline management" — *"realized we don't know which deals close this quarter."*

**2 — Score (15 min, quarterly).**

| CEP | Frequency: High / Med / Low | Association: Strong / Weak / None |

Strong = published content, ads or testimonials addressing that exact moment.
None = invisible.

**3 — Prioritize.** High frequency × Weak/None = the opportunities. Pick 2–3 per
quarter, each with one concrete action.

**4 — Re-score quarterly.** Add CEPs from new JTBD data, retire the rare ones.

At scale, re-map **per segment** before expanding. Segment B has never heard of you
and links the category to different CEPs.

## Physical availability audit

Score Good / Needs work / Missing. **More than 3 gaps in a section makes it the
constraint.** Run quarterly, and any time acquisition looks healthy but activation
doesn't — that pattern is usually this, not the product.

**Findable** — rank for the words a buyer types when the moment hits (the problem,
not your name)? Listed on the review sites and directories for the category, with
recent reviews? Present in its marketplaces? Showing up for "[category] vs
[competitor]"? Can a customer share you easily?

**Tryable** — start in under 2 minutes with no human? Minutes to first real value?
Is pricing visible (hidden pricing loses buyers silently)? Any way to experience it
before paying?

**Buyable** — card without a sales call? Integrates with what's already in their
stack? Enterprise: SSO, SOC 2, standard MSAs, procurement portals.

## Positioning sprint

One week. Run it before any further marketing spend.

**1. Best-fit customers (1–2h).** The 3–5 who got the most *value*, not revenue.
Used it most, renewed fastest, referred someone. Mixed segments? Take the one with
the shortest cycle, highest retention, clearest job. **Pick one.**

**2. Interview them (2–3h).** Four questions, and **write their exact words**:
- "What were you doing before you found us?"
- "What made you decide to try us?"
- "What would you tell a colleague about what we do?"
- "If we disappeared tomorrow, what would you miss most?"

**3. Find the thread (30 min).** Same struggling moment? Same job? Same words for
the value?

**4. Draft it (30 min).** *"For [segment], [brand] is the [category] that
[differentiator] because [reason to believe]."* Extract **two** associations — three
at the absolute most — in customer language.

**5. Kill everything that doesn't fit.** Every piece of content, feature request and
sales conversation: does it reinforce one of the two? If not it's noise. This step is
the discipline, and it's the one they won't do unless told.

**Distinctiveness is a separate job from differentiation.** Differentiation answers
"why choose you"; distinctiveness answers "which one is you." Pick 5–8 codes — logo,
colors, type, tone, tagline — use them relentlessly, and resist refreshing before
anything has been recognized. Test: show your content with the logo removed. Can
anyone tell?

**Narrow targeting is not narrow reach.** Sell to one segment; be known by everyone
who could ever be in it. Those are different dials — collapsing them is the error.

## Awareness check — before building anything

When they say "we need to build X":

1. **"Do enough of the right people know we exist?"** No → reach, content,
   partnerships, presence at CEPs. **Not a feature.**
2. **"Can they easily find, try and buy us?"** No → physical availability audit.
3. **"Are we distinctive enough to be remembered?"** No → brand assets, consistently.

**The one exception — features that ARE distribution.** Integrations that list you
in a partner's marketplace, sharing mechanics, embeddable widgets, public APIs.
Test: *"Will this bring visitors who'd never have found us otherwise?"* Yes → build it.

**Cadence when awareness is the constraint.** Weekly: did we publish? which CEPs did
it cover? how many *new* people saw us (reach, not engagement)? Monthly: CEP coverage
over 4 weeks, channel count (under 3 is under-diversified), does the month's output
look like one brand?

| Team | Weekly minimum | Channels |
|---|---|---|
| Solo | 1 short piece | 2 |
| 2–3 | 2 pieces | 3 |
| 4–5 | 3 pieces | 4 |

**Never go dark.** Consistency beats any individual piece. Keep some brand spend even
when activation is urgent — 100% activation makes every future dollar more expensive.

## Estimation and buffers

1. **Focused estimate** per task — *"how long with no interruptions?"* (~50%
   confidence). Use it as the duration. **Never pad tasks.**
2. **Critical chain** = longest dependent sequence including resource conflicts.
3. **Buffer = chain × 0.4**, at the end. 20-day chain → 8-day buffer → commit day 28.
4. **Schedule as late as possible.** Reduces WIP.
5. **Relay race** — next person starts on handoff, not on the calendar.
6. **Track buffer consumption, never task deadlines.**

**Only the buffer end date is a commitment.** Never a point estimate externally. If
someone demands one date, give the buffer end date — that's what it's for.

| Work | Multiplier |
|---|---|
| Repeatable, done it before | 0.3 |
| Normal startup work | **0.4 — the default** |
| Novel, external dependencies, regulatory | 0.5 |

**Fever chart** — % work done vs % buffer consumed. Green <1/3 (don't fill slack with
scope). Yellow 1/3–2/3 (normal — the buffer is working). Red >2/3 (act: cut scope,
pull in non-constraint capacity).

**After 3+ weeks of data, stop estimating and measure.** Median cycle time for
planning; 85th percentile for external commitments.

**Sizing:** S = hours, M = 1–2 days, L = 3–5 days. **XL means break it down** — you
don't understand it well enough to build it. Anything over 3 days is too big.

**Filter first:** is this work on the constraint? Yes → estimate carefully. No →
T-shirt it and move on.

## Premortem

Before any multi-week commitment. Imagining an outcome has already happened improves
the reasons people can identify for it.

> "It's six months from now. This failed completely. Write down why."

Silent and individual first, then read out. The founder goes last — otherwise you get
their answer echoed back.

## Calibration scoring

The reason this exists: startups are an environment where feedback is delayed, noisy
and confounded, so experience alone never corrects a founder's confidence. Well-
prepared and poorly-prepared founders are equally sure. Scoring is the only fix, and
under an hour a year of it measurably improves forecasting.

Every experiment carries a **written prediction and a confidence number** before it
runs. On return, score it. Quarterly, show them the tally:

- **40–60% of predictions right** — calibrated.
- **Over 70%** — predicting only safe things. Push for harder calls.
- **Under 30%** — overconfident. Widen the ranges.

This is also the mechanism that decides whether a founder gains or loses from working
with you: what separates people who benefit from AI advice is the habit of eliciting,
filtering and **verifying** — which is precisely predict-then-check, practised weekly.
