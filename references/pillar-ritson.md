# Ritson — Marketing Strategy Discipline

Load when positioning blocks constraint work: they can't say who they're for, why
someone picks them, or who they're *not* targeting.

You know the Mini MBA material. This file is how Factory Floor holds it.

---

## The mindset

- **Diagnosis → Strategy → Tactics, in that order.** The order is the whole
  discipline. Skipping ahead is the single biggest source of wasted marketing effort.
- **They are multiplicative, not additive.** A brilliant tactic on zero strategy
  produces zero. Say it that way.
- **Strategy is choices, not recommendations** — and every choice has a "won't"
  side. If nothing was ruled out, no strategy was made.
- **Refusing to choose a target is avoidance dressed as ambition.** "Everyone who
  needs X" is the diagnosis, not the answer.
- **Consistency beats creativity.** A mediocre position defended five years beats a
  brilliant one changed annually. The urge to refresh before recognition exists is
  the enemy.
- **They cannot see the product the way customers see it.** Neither can you. Until
  the team accepts that, every framework becomes self-confirmation.
- **12-month planning horizon.** Finance can plan five years out; marketing can't.

---

## The diagnostic

Five questions. Any stumble tells you the problem is strategy, not execution — and
strategy gets fixed first.

1. **"State your position in one sentence, in the customer's words."** Stumbling or
   listing several things → no positioning.
2. **"What two associations do you want to own?"** More than four, or no answer →
   no positioning discipline.
3. **"Who are you NOT targeting?"** "Everyone who…" or no segment they're ignoring
   → no targeting discipline.
4. **"When did you last talk to a customer who didn't buy?"** Months ago → no
   market orientation.
5. **"What share of marketing is brand vs. activation?"** Don't know, or 100%
   activation → budget imbalance.

## Positioning rules

- **A position is not a tagline.** It's the 2-4 associations you want to own —
  and fewer is better. "Fast, reliable, innovative, customer-obsessed, fun" is
  positioning for zero.
- **It's relative.** "Premium" means nothing unless someone else is budget.
- **Write it in the customer's words**, never marketing speak. What would they
  actually say to a friend?
- **Features are proof points, not position.**
- **You don't need to be the only one. You need to be chosen.**

Template: *"For [segment], [brand] is the [category] that [differentiator] because
[reason to believe]."*

Then pick **two** associations. Every communication reinforces at least one. Kill
whatever reinforces neither — that part is the discipline, and it's the part they
won't do without being told.

## Differentiation and distinctiveness are different jobs

**Differentiation** answers *"why should I choose you?"* — meaning, relative,
2-4 associations, requires strategic choice.

**Distinctiveness** answers *"which one is you?"* — the sensory codes (logo,
colors, shapes, typography, tone, tagline, characters, sounds) that trigger recall
without the name present.

Test each code on two axes: does it trigger the brand, and does it trigger *only*
your brand? Keep the ones scoring on both; the generic ones need replacing.

**The startup trap:** all effort on differentiation ("we're better because…"), none
on distinctive assets. Every pitch sounds the same, every site looks the same, no
memory forms. Pick 5-8 codes early, use them relentlessly, resist refreshing before
anything has been recognized.

**Informal test:** show your content with the logo removed. Can anyone tell it's you?

## Budget: the long and the short

~60% brand / 40% activation for established brands. **Startups tilt toward
activation — but never 100%.** Founders over-correct, run pure activation, and
stall.

Brand building lifts short-term sales too; activation almost never builds long-term
brand. When you cut brand spend, activation gets more expensive because you're
harvesting demand without replenishing it.

**Keep the two separate in reporting.** Awareness constraint → brand work → measure
reach. Conversion constraint → activation → measure conversions. Different work,
different metrics, different time horizon.

## The Positioning Sprint

One week, not a three-month project. Run it before any more marketing spend.

**1. Find the best-fit customers (1-2 h).** The 3-5 who got the most *value* — not
the most revenue. Who used it most, renewed fastest, referred someone. Mixed
segments? Ask which has the shortest sales cycle, highest retention, and clearest
job. **Pick one.**

**2. Interview them (2-3 h).** Four questions:
- "What were you doing before you found us?"
- "What made you decide to try us?"
- "What would you tell a colleague about what we do?"
- "If we disappeared tomorrow, what would you miss most?"

**Write their exact words. Never paraphrase.**

**3. Find the thread (30 min).** Same struggling moment? Same job? Same words for
the value?

**4. Draft the position (30 min).** Use the template. Extract two associations, in
customer language.

**5. Kill everything that doesn't fit (ongoing).** Every piece of content, feature
request, and sales conversation: does it reinforce one of the two? If not, it's noise.

---

## Market orientation

Before any of it works, the team has to accept they don't know what customers think.

Get into the field and shut up. Run qual before deciding what to measure. Confront
the team with **verbatims, not summaries** — the dissonance is the point. Ban the
phrase "we know our customers" and replace it with "what do our customers actually
think?"
