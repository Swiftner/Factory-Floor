# Strategic Thinking — Rumelt, Clausewitz, Dixit & Nalebuff

Load when the problem is strategic rather than operational:

- They have a plan that isn't actually a strategy.
- Paralyzed by uncertainty, can't commit to a direction.
- Overextended and don't know when to stop pushing.
- Facing a competitor and don't know how to respond.
- Thinking through a negotiation, pricing decision, or partnership.
- The team is demoralized despite reasonable metrics.

If the question is "which funnel step is broken," use the operational pillars. If
it's "do we have a real strategy," start here.

You know all three sources. This file is how Factory Floor holds them.

---

## The mindset

- **Bad strategy is an active thing, not an absence.** Fluff, never naming the
  obstacle, goals dressed as strategy, a laundry list of disconnected objectives.
  When you see one, **name which one it is** — then ask what the actual obstacle is.
- **Its root cause is conflict avoidance.** Bad strategy is usually an unwillingness
  to make a choice that disappoints someone. Treat it as a courage problem, not an
  analysis problem.
- **The hard part is figuring out what's going on, not deciding what to do.** Most
  founders skip straight to action because diagnosis feels like inaction.
- **Pick the challenge that's important AND addressable.** "The market isn't ready"
  may be true and is not addressable. Addressability is the filter founders skip.
- **The fog never lifts — it shifts.** Waiting for certainty is not caution, it's
  paralysis. Design an experiment that reduces uncertainty in one specific place
  rather than seeking general clarity.
- **Friction is cumulative, not singular.** Every parallel initiative multiplies the
  surfaces friction can act on. This is a strategic argument for WIP limits.
- **Concentration of force is the most-violated principle in startups**, and it is
  the same thing as subordination.
- **Moral forces decide outcomes more often than material ones.** A burnt-out team
  won't execute any strategy. Check founder conviction alongside the metrics.
- **Most startup games are positive-sum and repeated.** Playing them zero-sum and
  one-shot — price wars, burned bridges, aggressive negotiation — is a strategy
  error, not toughness.

## Testing whether it's a strategy

Three elements. Missing any one means it isn't a strategy.

1. **Diagnosis** — does it name the critical obstacle, as an interpretive frame
   rather than a list of facts?
2. **Guiding policy** — is there an approach that directs and constrains action
   without fully specifying it? Not a goal. Not a vision.
3. **Coherent actions** — do the moves reinforce each other, or merely coexist?

This maps directly onto Ritson's Diagnosis → Strategy → Tactics. Rumelt is
domain-general; Ritson is marketing-specific. Use whichever the founder will hear.

**Then set a proximate objective:** close enough to be feasible with current skills
and resources, phrased as a task rather than an aspiration. **The leader's job is
to absorb complexity and hand the team a solvable problem.** How proximate it needs
to be depends on who they are and how uncertain things look — the more fog, the
more proximate.

The proximate objective *is* the next GOLEAN experiment.

## Coaching moves

**"Is this actually a strategy?"** → run the three-element test above and name what's missing.

**When they can't commit** → "What experiment would reduce the uncertainty in one
specific place?" Not general clarity — one place.

**When they're overextended** → *"Are you still gaining ground, or just burning more
to hold position?"* Past the culminating point, more pushing produces vulnerability,
not gains. The correct response is consolidation: stop the advance, secure what you
hold, rebuild. Common forms — expanding to three segments after fit in one, feature
bloat for edge cases while core users churn, hiring ahead of revenue.

**When a bigger competitor enters** → defense is the stronger form. Deepen the
niche and let them overextend. Runway *is* defense. Attack when fit is confirmed,
retention proves it, and you can see where they're stretched.

**Competitor inertia is your advantage** — their processes, culture, and defense of
declining revenue are structural and slow. Ask what they *can't* do rather than
what they haven't done.

**Before any sequential decision** (hiring, fundraising, launch) → *"If this works,
what happens next? If it doesn't, what are we left with?"* Founders who think one
move ahead get trapped.

**Before any negotiation** → *"What's your best alternative if this falls through?"*
Strengthen that first; it's where all the leverage lives.

**On commitment** → *"What would you need to commit to, publicly and irreversibly,
for this plan to work?"* A pricing page, a published date, a specific hire. Keeping
every option open signals to customers and partners that nothing is certain, which
slows deals. Commitment accelerates by removing everyone else's uncertainty.

**On terms and tiers** → uniform terms attract the worst fits. Free-forever attracts
people who'll never pay; below-market comp "made up in equity" attracts people who
can't get market-rate offers. Design terms that screen for the type you want.

**On being first** → *"Are you first because you see something others don't, or
because nobody else thinks this is worth doing?"* The first is an advantage. The
second is a warning.

## Alignment replaces control

**Naming the constraint is an alignment act.** When everyone knows "our constraint
is activation — people sign up and don't come back after day one," each person can
evaluate their own work against it. When it's unnamed, everything feels equally
important and every decision routes through the founder.

| Mechanism | What it aligns |
|---|---|
| Subordination | Everyone knows the constraint and orients to it |
| Guiding policy | Guardrails that constrain without micromanaging |
| Proximate objective | Leader absorbs complexity; team gets a solvable problem |
| Two associations | Everyone knows what we stand for |
| The job statement | One sentence naming what customers hire us for |
| The assigned experiment | Committed, then the founder gets out of the way |
| Weekly review | The constraint is re-confirmed or moved on a cadence |
| WIP limits | A structural limit that prevents drift without oversight |

**The anti-pattern:** the founder who must be involved in everything has an
alignment problem, not a delegation problem. The fix is never "trust your team
more." It's: name the constraint, state the guiding policy, assign the experiment,
get out of the way.

**Coaching move:** *"Can everyone on your team name the current constraint? Can
they say who you're building for and why? If not — that's the problem. Not their
capability. Your clarity."*
